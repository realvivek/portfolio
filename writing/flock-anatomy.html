building the flock camera reference

Flock Safety license plate readers are on more than 96,000 documented poles in the United States, and the public argument about them runs mostly on claims nobody checks. Anatomy of a Flock Camera is a nine-page reference that puts the checkable record in one place: the hardware part by part, the data path from capture to deletion, the contracts, the published outcomes, and twenty-one common claims tested against cited documents. This is how it was built in twenty days, what the hardware taught me about the cellular IoT market, and what broke along the way.

VIVEK YALAMANCHILI · BUILT WITH CLAUDE FABLE 5 · VITE + TYPESCRIPT · THREE.JS · BLENDER · PLAYWRIGHT

ground rules

Five rules held for the whole project. Every number on the site lives in a JSON content file, and every entry carries a sources array pointing into one bibliography that grew from about 60 rows to 176. Each source is tagged by origin (company, independent, government, or court) so a reader can tell a Flock statement from an independently documented one; where they disagree, both are cited. The build refuses to run if a claim cites a source id that does not exist or a listed still image is missing. Copy stays neutral: a camera that reads a plate "reads a plate", and an audit that found 71% of alerts wrong is reported with its denominator and the agency's own explanation. Nothing ships unverified: TypeScript typecheck, production build, a five-test Playwright suite, and a viewport sweep of every page at eight sizes, with zero findings required before every push.

the 3d pipeline

The models are parametric. Blender scripts read the same content files as the site and emit four GLB files; the Falcon's fourteen parts are named nodes, so a corrected measurement in the content changes the geometry and the exploded view follows. Enclosure, pole, and panel dimensions are Flock's own published figures; parts marked "estimated" are sized from the published envelope and teardown photographs. The same models render 26 stills with Cycles. Phones never download a 3D engine: they get the stills and about 60 KB of JavaScript. Desktops get WebGPU where available, WebGL2 otherwise, and fall back to the stills if no engine starts. A source checker refuses to build if a listed still is missing, so the manifest and the images cannot drift apart.

the lte chip, and why iot does not want 5g

The most instructive part in the enclosure is the modem. The Falcon's sole data path is an LTE module (a Sierra Wireless RC76-series on current units, FCC ID N7NRC76B; a NimbeLink Skywire on older ones) with a carrier SIM included in the subscription. There is no wired network interface. Public sources disagree on whether it runs LTE Cat-1 or Cat-4, and the site records that dispute rather than resolving it. Either way, a camera sold on AI-powered vehicle intelligence moves its data over a radio generation that launched in 2009, and that is not a compromise. It is the correct engineering choice, and the module market says so.

A solar-and-battery device that uploads plate crops and metadata needs tens of kilobits per second sustained, single-digit watts peak, a module that costs a few dollars, and coverage on any rural roadside. LTE Cat-1 delivers all four; 5G delivers none of them at comparable cost or power. The industry shipped accordingly. In 2025, global cellular IoT module shipments grew 15% year over year, and LTE Cat-1 bis alone accounted for roughly half of all shipments, the single largest technology in the market (Counterpoint Research). Add the other LTE categories still shipping at volume (Cat-1, Cat-M, Cat-4 and above) and LTE-family modules made up on the order of 60% of cellular IoT module shipments last year, while 5G, RedCap included, stayed in the single digits and NB-IoT eased back from its 2024 peak (IoT Analytics). The forecasts point the same way: Cat-1 bis is projected to remain the volume leader through 2030, with RedCap only beginning its ramp.

The general lesson: IoT demand is for cheap, low-power, good-enough links with mature coverage, not peak throughput. For anyone building connected infrastructure, the Falcon is a working example of the market's revealed preference. The premium radio is not the one doing the volume.

One correction from the research is worth recording. The modem citation originally pointed at FCC grantee code 2BMEK, which turned out to belong to an unrelated gauge company. Flock's own grantee code, 2BKG8, was registered in August 2024 and has no filings: the Falcon uses pre-certified radio modules, so the product itself never appears in the FCC's database. The point survived; the citation was replaced.

the outcomes page

The hardest page asked what the cameras produce, not where they are. Three research surveys (about 300 searches, about 45 reports read) found that almost nothing ties a specific camera to a specific outcome in public. What exists: one per-site table anywhere in the country (Metro Nashville's 2023 pilot report: 443 verified hits, 25 stops, 18 arrests, 22 recoveries across 24 intersections), one county's export of hot-list hits reviewed as wrong (Story County, Iowa: 214 rows, 77% "wrong state"), one city's dispatch layer with a FLOCK nature code (Tucson), a Connecticut town's fact sheet naming cameras in ten cases, a court opinion naming one camera, and a news-derived dataset of 1,743 reported outcomes. Every located record was geocoded and joined by haversine distance to the nearest of 96,484 mapped Flock cameras from the hourly OpenStreetMap export; a record counts as matched within 150 metres. Every rung a source did not report is shown as "not reported", never as zero, and the page states in numbers that most mapped cameras have no public outcome record at all.

Geocoding was the fight. Nominatim cannot resolve street intersections; Overpass instances reset connections or timed out at ten minutes per failed road; and two innocent query-shape changes invalidated every cache key, turning cache hits back into 504s. The fix was to normalise cache lookups on the semantic query rather than its literal text, and to add an offline mode that builds from caches alone: 220 of 239 locations placed, the 19 unresolved labelled as such on the page.

what else broke

  • The site's shape changed four times (one long scroll, tabs, one document, separate pages) and every earlier deep link still resolves, so nothing shared early ever broke.
  • An exploded view with fourteen leader-lined callouts was judged unreadable. Ten alternative mockups later, a knolling grid won: parts at consistent scale in five groups, with a see-through locator beside it.
  • A build-environment proxy hung the browser's load event on third-party font requests, stalling tests for hours until every automated navigation waited for commit instead of load.
  • The current-stop rule on the journey page was wrong three times at different viewport heights before a rule combining header position and scroll fraction survived checks at eight sizes.

tooling

The site was built in Claude Code sessions running Claude Fable 5, working against the rules above: content changes gated by the source checker, larger changes planned and approved before implementation, and zero sweep findings before every push. The repository itself stays authored by me; the model is credited here and on the project card rather than in the commit history.
